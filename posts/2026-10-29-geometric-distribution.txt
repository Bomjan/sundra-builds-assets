You roll a die six times. No six.

Surely the next roll is finally your turn?

The die has no memory. If it is fair and the rolls are independent, the next roll still has a 1-in-6 chance of landing on six.

Here is the surprising part: the chance of getting no six in your first six rolls is about 33.5%.

That is roughly one in three attempts at this six-roll experiment. Not especially strange at all.

The calculation is small: each roll has a 5-in-6 chance of missing. For six independent misses, multiply 5/6 by itself six times.

The number of rolls you wait for your first six follows a geometric distribution. Its average is six rolls, but an average is not a deadline. Some waits end immediately; others run much longer.

This matters in code too. If you simulate independent attempts with a fixed chance of success, repeated failures do not make the next attempt more likely to succeed. Real systems can behave differently when conditions change or attempts are linked.

Randomness does not owe you a win.
