You add one more item to a list. Suddenly, it copies every item already there.

How can anyone call that fast?

Imagine a dynamic array that doubles its capacity whenever it fills up.

With four items in four slots, the fifth append needs a larger array. It copies the four existing items into eight slots, then stores the new item. That append is expensive.

But the next few appends use the spare room. They do not copy the whole array again.

Starting from capacity one, the resize copies form a pattern: 1, 2, 4, 8, and so on. Across n appends from empty, those copies total less than 2n. Add the n new-item writes, and the overall work is still O(n).

That is O(1) amortized cost per append, even though an individual resizing append can cost O(n).

“Amortized” spreads the total cost across the sequence. It does not promise that every operation is quick, and it does not depend on lucky random inputs.

Java’s ArrayList also guarantees amortized constant-time append, but its API does not promise this exact doubling policy.

A rare expensive operation can be affordable when it buys enough cheap ones afterward.
